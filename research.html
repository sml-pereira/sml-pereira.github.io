Research

My research develops the Fourier Qualia Space (FQS), a framework that uses Fourier transforms to follow how harmonic qualities such as chromaticity, octatonicity, diatonicity, and whole-tone quality unfold over time and build hierarchical and syntactic structure. At its heart is qualia ambiguity: rather than dismissing it as analytical noise, the FQS treats it as a structural resource that composers use to shape form and tension.

The repertoire spans four centuries of Western art music, with a soft spot for the post-tonal works of Schoenberg, Debussy, Webern, and their neighbours, explored through both large-scale corpus analysis and close reading. Ambiguity, it turns out, is far easier to enjoy once you can measure it.

Qualia in Motion: Mapping Harmonic Qualities with the Discrete Fourier Transform

Seminar 1: Sound, Art and Technology, Master’s in Computer Music and Sound Design, University of Coimbra, Figueira da Foz campus.

Upcoming — 2 December 2026

How diatonic is a chord? How octatonic or whole-tone is a passage, and what happens when several of these qualities coexist? In this session, I present the Fourier Qualia Space, a geometrical framework I developed in my PhD for answering such questions computationally. The discrete Fourier transform, the same tool behind spectral analysis, is applied here not to sound signals but to pitch-class content, where its coefficients measure harmonic qualities such as diatonicity, octatonicity and whole-tone quality. These are then projected onto a hexagonal space where proximity indicates similar qualities and centrality indicates ambiguity between them. I will show how this space supports analysis at very different scales: from a single Schoenberg piano piece and hierarchical “wavescapes” of Bach, Debussy and Webern to a corpus of nearly 9,000 works from 1548 to 1968, processed in Python. The results trace the gradual dissolution of tonality, reveal Zipfian, language-like distributions in sequences of harmonic qualities, with Debussy as a paradigmatic case, and suggest that ambiguity is a compositional resource deployed strategically rather than an analytical problem. I will close with the questions this opens for analysis, composition and creative tools.

Tonfeld Theory and the Discrete Fourier Transform: A Bridge Between Hermeneutic and Mathematical Accounts of Extended Tonality

EnIM 26: 15th Meeting on Music Research, Royal Building of Mafra, Mafra, Portugal.

Tonfeld theory (Tonfeldtheorie), conceived by Albert Simon and later disseminated by Bernhard Haas (2004), explains musical coherence in a repertoire stretching from Schubert and Liszt to Debussy, Bartók, and even the Second Viennese School. Instead of relying on a tonic or on voice leading, it posits that pitches cohere as elements of three types of structured pitch fields, namely, the fifths-rows (Quintenreihen), the hexatonic Konstrukt, and the octatonic Funktion. Despite a rich German-language reception, and ramifications reaching jazz, film, and minimal music, the theory remains largely unknown internationally and has rarely been integrated with other analytical frameworks. This paper therefore first introduces the fundamental categories and characteristic effects of Tonfeld theory (the equivalence effect within a Funktion, the contrastive effect of the Konstrukt, enharmonic ambivalence, and “twice-twelve-tone” backgrounds), and argues that its analytical principles offer a distinctive perspective on extended tonality and imply a revised historiography of the transition to atonality. It then enquires to what extent this hermeneutically grounded theory can be reconstructed within the mathematical framework of the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of pitch-class sets (Amiot, 2016; Quinn, 2006, 2007; Yust, 2016). The paper shows that Simon’s three field types align with the prototype collections of the fifth, third, and fourth DFT coefficients (diatonicity, hexatonicity, and octatonicity) at their respective cardinalities. Additionally, core operations of the theory (root equivalence within a Funktion, complementarity, authentic/plagal function change, and enharmonic ambivalence) admit formal DFT counterparts in transpositional symmetries, complement-invariant magnitudes, and phase rotations. This correspondence is then tested analytically on Bartók’s Staccato (Mikrokosmos no. 124) and on a structural hinge of Divided Arpeggios (no. 143), read through both Tonfeld methods and the DFT, with Simon’s own analyses of these pieces as benchmark (Haas, 2011). The paper concludes by showing that a proposed extension of the field inventory, a whole-tone field (Winkler, 2023), is already predicted by the Fourier framework, and outlines a research programme connecting the German-Hungarian tradition of extended tonality with contemporary mathematical music theory.

The Fourier Qualia Space: Interaction, Ambiguity, and Hierarchy in Music Harmony

11th European Music Analysis Conference (EuroMAC11), Escola Superior de Música de Catalunya (ESMUC), Barcelona.

Slides (PDF)

This paper presents the Fourier Qualia Space (FQS), a geometric framework that reconceptualises harmonic qualities as harmonic qualia, i.e., measurable relationships within mathematically determined space. Whilst music theory has long recognised harmonic qualities such as diatonicity and octatonicity, existing frameworks provide no unified method for measuring their relative proportions, representing them within ambiguous passages, or comparing their deployment across repertoires that currently demand separate methodologies.

The FQS addresses these limitations through the discrete Fourier transform, which converts pitch-class sets into coefficients capturing six specific harmonic qualities (Amiot 2016; Yust 2016). Dimensional reduction then maps these coefficients onto a hexagonal space where proximity signals qualia resemblance and centrality denotes ambiguity. Building upon Quinn’s (2006–2007) pioneering work on quality space, this geometric representation enables analysis at multiple scales: from phrase-level examination through hierarchical wavescape constructions to large-scale corpus analysis.

The methodology was applied across four centuries of Western music. Results confirmed progressive dissolution of traditional tonal structures whilst revealing how alternative organisational principles emerged: (1) qualia sequences converged progressively towards Zipfian distributions characteristic of efficient communication systems; furthermore, (2) close analysis of Schoenberg’s Op. 19/1 demonstrated how the composer achieves coherence without traditional syntax through systematic deployment of qualia ambiguity; and finally, extending the statistical approach pioneered by Rohrmeier and Cross (2008), (3) statistical analysis of Debussy’s harmonic practice identified three roles—anchor, transitional, and colouristic qualities—governing qualia deployment, demonstrating that his music operates through identifiable organisational principles analogous to, yet fundamentally different from, tonal grammar.

These findings carry broader implications for music theory. Most significantly, qualia ambiguity emerges not as analytical indeterminacy but as a compositional resource deployed strategically across the repertoires examined. The identification of language-like patterns in post-tonal repertoires suggests that composers working without traditional syntactic constraints nonetheless deployed harmonic qualities according to measurable principles—a hypothesis the FQS framework can now test across stylistic boundaries previously demanding entirely separate methodologies.

The Fourier Qualia Space: Interaction, Ambiguity, and Hierarchy in Music Harmony

PhD Defence, Faculty of Engineering, University of Porto.

Slides (PDF)

Music theory has long recognised harmonic qualities and developed tools for their analytical identification, yet existing frameworks provide no unified method for measuring their relative proportions, representing them within ambiguous passages, or comparing their deployment across distinct repertoires that currently demand separate methodologies. These limitations manifest differently across analytical traditions. Tonal analysis captures functional relationships effectively, yet falters when triads or diatonic collections appear without the progressions that would render them functional. Set theory provides rigorous measurement through interval vectors and set-class membership, yet fails to explain why two sonorities sound different whilst sharing identical interval vectors. What unites these shortcomings is a common conceptual gap: none of these frameworks treats ambiguity—passages where multiple harmonic qualities coexist—as a compositional resource.

To address these limitations, this thesis developed the Fourier Qualia Space, a geometrical framework that reconceptualises harmonic qualities as measurable qualia relationships within mathematically determined space. The discrete Fourier transform converts pitch-class sets into coefficients capturing specific harmonic qualities, which dimensional reduction then projects into a hexagonal space where proximity signals qualia resemblance and centrality denotes ambiguity. This geometric representation enables analysis at multiple scales, ranging from phrase-level examination of Schoenberg’s Op. 19/1, through hierarchical wavescape constructions for Bach, Debussy, and Webern, to diachronic corpus analysis spanning 1548–1968.

Applying this methodology across four centuries of Western music confirmed progressive dissolution of traditional tonal structures and provided novel quantitative evidence for how alternative organisational principles emerged: qualia sequences converged progressively towards Zipfian distributions characteristic of efficient communication systems. Among composers whose distributions most closely approximate this pattern, Debussy emerged as a paradigmatic case: statistical analysis of his harmonic practice identified three systematic roles governing qualia deployment, demonstrating that his music operates through identifiable organisational principles. Most significantly for the conceptual reframing proposed at the outset, qualia ambiguity functions not as analytical indeterminacy but as a compositional resource deployed strategically across the repertoires examined.

These findings carry broader implications. The identification of language-like patterns in Debussy’s deployment of harmonic qualia exemplifies what the Fourier Qualia Space makes possible: if one composer working without tonal syntax constraints nonetheless deployed harmonic qualities according to measurable principles, others may have done likewise—a hypothesis the framework can now test across stylistic boundaries that previously demanded entirely separate methodologies.

Qualia Motion in Fourier Space: Formalising Linear, Nondirected and Contrapuntal Ambiguity in Schoenberg's Op. 19, No. 1

Music as Interdiscipline Colloquium (MIC), University of Coimbra and CEIS20, with Richard Cohn.

Slides (PDF)

In this presentation, we formalise and analyse qualia motion, i.e., the process by which a composition transitions across distinct harmonic qualities through the Fourier qualia space (FQS)—a multidimensional and transposition-independent space based on the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) coefficients’ magnitude. In the FQS, the plot of set classes relies on their harmonic qualities—such as diatonicity and octatonicity—enabling us to (1) identify the pitch-class set in a musical phrase that best represents its qualia—a reference sonority; (2) define a harmonic progression using all sequential reference sonorities in a piece; (3) visualise trajectory in space; and (4) establish a statistical metric for the ambiguity of harmonic qualia. Finally, we discuss Schoenberg’s Op. 19, No. 1, analysing the sense of its harmonic path. The proposed space leverages a bipartite, symmetrical, and consequential structure and unveils ambiguity as an element of nondirected linearity and counterpoint.

Historical Evolution of Harmonic Qualities in Music: Unravelling Fourier’s Tonality, Entropy, and Ambiguity from the 16th to the 20th Centuries

Digital Technologies Applied to Music Research (DTAMR24), NOVA University, Lisbon.

Slides (PDF)

The assessment of temporal evolution is a recurrent methodology in computational musicology that provides valuable insights into the lexical and syntactic structures that have shaped diverse aesthetics over centuries. This understanding serves as a historical window through which we can examine the musical phenomenon, bridging the gap between historical context and contemporary analysis. In this study, we delve into the evolution of the six non-trivial discrete Fourier transform coefficients, alongside with three distinct harmonic metrics: the tonal index, the entropy of Fourier coefficients magnitude, and qualia ambiguity. Our investigation spans the last 500 years, offering a unique perspective on the interplay and transformation of harmonic qualities within musical repertoires. To this end, we construct our analysis using a symbolic dataset sourced from the Yale-Classical Archives Corpus and supplemented by compositions from 20th-century composers. By scrutinising this collection of works, we explore how harmonic qualities—such as chromaticity, diadicity, triadicity/hexatonicity, octatonicity, diatonicity, and whole-tone quality—interact and evolve. Furthermore, we unveil the dataset’s proximity to a functional tonal discourse (with the tonal index), the degree of structure or randomness in their pitch-class distributions (the entropy of the Fourier coefficients), and the inherent ambiguity of the musical discourse within the realm of harmonic qualities (qualia ambiguity). Our findings corroborate previously reported notions, including the gradual increase in average cardinality over the centuries and the tendency towards a tonal index close to zero (tonal index ≈ 0) in functional tonal discourses. Additionally, we quantify relatively stable levels of qualia ambiguity during the 18th and 19th centuries, contrasting with significant fluctuations observed in the 16th, 17th, and 20th centuries. Finally, we highlight a strong correlation between the entropy of the Fourier coefficients and qualia ambiguity, and we discuss works where both metrics misalign.

Fourier Qualia Wavescapes: Hierarchical Analyses of Set Class Quality and Ambiguity

Mathematics and Computation in Music (MCM24), Faculty of Arts and Humanities,University of Coimbra.

Slides (PDF)

We introduce a novel perspective on set-class analysis combining the DFT magnitudes with the music visualisation technique of wavescapes. With such a combination, we create a visual representation of a piece’s multidimensional qualia, where different colours indicate saliency in chromaticity, diadicity, triadicity, octatonicity, diatonicity, and whole-tone quality. At the centre of our methods are: 1) the formal definition of the Fourier Qualia Space (FQS), 2) its particular ordering of DFT coefficients that delineate regions linked to different musical aesthetics, and 3) the mapping of such regions into a coloured wavescape. Furthermore, we demonstrate the intrinsic capability of the FQS to express qualia ambiguity and map it into a synopsis wavescape. Finally, we showcase the application of our methods by presenting a few analytical remarks on Bach’s Three-part Invention BWV 795, Debussy’s Reflets dans l’eau, and Webern’s Four Pieces for Violin and Piano, Op. 7, No. 1, unveiling increasingly ambiguous wavescapes.

A Propósito da Representação do Movimento Musical: o Tonnetz e os Espaços de Fourier

XI Encontro Nacional de Investigação em Música (ENIM), University of Aveiro.

Slides (PDF)

O problema da visualização do movimento musical, nomeadamente das suas progressões harmónicas, está, há muito, identificado na literatura. Duas das abordagens para a sua resolução, o Tonnetz e os espaços de fase derivados da transformada discreta de Fourier para conjuntos de classes de altura, desenvolvem uma visão das estruturas musicais verticais como objetos harmónicos representados no domínio de um espaço geométrico. Assim, através de um breve estudo comparativo, pretende-se refletir nesta comunicação, a propósito das especificidades geométricas destes espaços, da sua construção e principais características, mas também a propósito das suas limitações e potencialidades futuras. Neste âmbito, a possibilidade de representar qualquer cardinalidade é identificada na literatura como uma das principais vantagens associadas aos espaços de fase, carecendo estes, ainda, de representações multidimensionais que tirem proveito de toda a informação relevante da transformada discreta de Fourier.

English version

The problem of visualising musical movement, namely its harmonic progressions, has long been identified in the literature. Two of the approaches to solving it, the Tonnetz and the phase spaces derived from the discrete Fourier transform of pitch-class sets, develop a view of vertical musical structures as harmonic objects represented within the domain of a geometric space. Through a brief comparative study, this paper therefore aims to reflect on the geometric specificities of these spaces, their construction and main characteristics, but also on their limitations and future potential. In this context, the ability to represent any cardinality is identified in the literature as one of the main advantages of phase spaces, although these still lack multidimensional representations that take advantage of all the relevant information in the discrete Fourier transform.

A Sintaxe Harmónica Atonal no Espaço de Fourier

X Encontro Nacional de Investigação em Música (ENIM), University of Coimbra.

Slides (PDF)

Os arquétipos da sintaxe harmónica e a função que desempenham dentro da estrutura temporal da música tonal ocidental estão amplamente sistematizados nos tratados de teoria musical enquanto princípios baseados em regras. Por exemplo, a função que um acorde desempenha dentro das estruturas tonais é tipicamente reduzida a uma categorização tripla (tónica, subdominante e dominante), cujo alinhamento temporal comporta significado. Na música pós-tonal do início do século XX, este fenómeno é muito menos sistematizado e compreendido, evoluindo mesmo, nos últimos anos, para a existência de quase tantas teorias e sistemas de composição como o número de compositores ou de obras. Neste contexto, o presente estudo avança alguns métodos e ferramentas dentro da musicologia computacional, que visam encontrar padrões sintáticos (i.e., temporais) típicos em estruturas harmónicas atonais. Para tal, seguem-se métodos recentes de Yust sobre a utilização da informação de fase a partir da transformada discreta de Fourier para conjuntos de classes de altura. O principal contributo deste estudo, ampliando investigações anteriores, é uma representação esférica tridimensional na qual foi disposta a informação de fase de dois coeficientes de ℤ12 com magnitude saliente, para traçar as dimensões temporais dos conjuntos de classes de altura de uma peça atonal. Os mapeamentos espaciais daí resultantes identificam automaticamente agregados de conteúdo intervalar, bem como os padrões harmónicos típicos formados a partir dos mesmos. Finalmente, para testar esta representação tridimensional, a mesma foi utilizada para analisar a obra Sechs kleine Klavierstücke, Op. 19, de Arnold Schoenberg, e também como ferramenta de apoio ao processo de composição, sugerindo padrões de movimento no material harmónico apresentado pelo compositor. Em resumo, pretende-se com este estudo: (1) dispor os conjuntos harmónicos do Op. 19, de Arnold Schoenberg, num espaço tridimensional de acordo com o seu conteúdo intervalar; (2) organizar esses conjuntos em diferentes agregados; (3) identificar a existência de movimentos padronizados entre esses agregados; (4) comparar os resultados da representação com análises anteriores e (5) avaliar a utilidade do sistema no processo criativo do compositor. Os resultados mostram, entre outros fatores, que as trajetórias harmónicas padronizadas no espaço esférico de fases tendem, em cada repetição, a preencher as doze classes de altura da escala cromática.

English version

Harmonic syntax archetypes and the function they play within the temporal structure of Western tonal music is vastly systematised across music theory treatises as rule-based principles. E.g., the function a chord plays within tonal structures is typically reduced to a threefold categorisation (tonic, subdominant, and dominant), whose temporal alignment implies meaning. In post-tonal music since the early 20th century, this phenomenon is much less systematised and understood, evolving even in recent years, to the existence of almost as many theories and compositional systems as the number of composers or individual works. In this context, we advance some methods and tools within computational musicology, which aims to unpack typical syntactic (i.e. temporal) patterns in atonal harmonic structures. To this end, we follow recent methods by Yust on the adoption of the phase information from the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of pitch class sets. Our contribution extends previous research by proposing a three-dimensional spherical representation resulting from the phase information of two ℤ12 magnitude-salient coefficients to plot the temporal dimensions of pitch class sets within an atonal music piece. Resulting spatial mappings automatically identify interval-content clusters and typical harmonic patterns across them. Finally, to test this three dimensional representation, we use it to analyse Sechs kleine Klavierstücke, Op. 19, by Arnold Schoenberg and as an assistant to the composing process, suggesting movement patterns in the harmonic material presented by the composer. In summary, we intend with this study to: (1) plot the harmonic sets of Arnold Schoenberg’s Op. 19 in a three-dimensional space according to its interval content; (2) group such sets into different clusters; (3) identify the existence of standardised movements between these clusters; (4) compare the results of the representation with previous analysis and (5) assess the utility of the system in the composer’s creative process. The results, among others, advance standardised harmonic paths within the spherical phase space that, with each repetition, tend to fill all twelve pitch classes of the chromatic scale.

Harmonic Cartographies and the Conceptualisation of Musical Movement: A Syntax for Atonal Music in Fourier Space

DMDS 2021/22: Digital Media Doctoral Symposium, Rectorate of the University of Porto, Porto.

Musical syntax, along with other linguistic metaphors such as phonetics and semantics, is a fundamental element in attributing meaning to musical structures. For example, harmonic syntax concerns the principles governing the combination of discrete musical elements in the formation of vertical structures, such as pitch-class sets (pcsets), or horizontal ones, i.e. harmonic progressions (Patel, 2010; Wiggins, Müllensiefen & Pearce, 2010; Rohrmeier & Pearce, 2018). This concept has been extensively studied in the context of tonal music (Patel, 2010), clearly and systematically identifying multiple levels of predictability in its harmonic structures. However, the empirical understanding of harmonic syntax in atonalism (Perle, 1991) still lacks investigation and systematisation.

Consequently, the purpose of this project is to investigate whether the temporal evolution of harmonic qualities—such as chromaticity or octatonicity—in atonal works, contains relations of continuity and predictability that interfere in their phrasing and their composers’ aesthetic plan. To analyse such relations, an empirical methodological approach is proposed. It systematically and computationally processes an extended set of these works and identifies patterns in the representation of harmonic qualities in a geometric space (Bernardes et al., 2016; Martins, 2009; Pereira, 2020). Thus, by transposing harmonic information from the score domain to that of a geometric space, metaphors often used to describe harmony, such as the distance between pcsets and harmonic trajectories/regions (Yust, 2015; Quinn, 2006–7), become clear and ultimately promote the investigation of atonal syntax. Therefore, one of the theoretical frameworks that allows such a transition is the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of pcsets (Lewin, 1959, 2001; Amiot, 2013, 2018), which underlies the most innovative, albeit embryonic, contributions of recent years in music analysis and cognition (Yust, 2015, 2016; Dawson, Perez & Sylvestre, 2020).

English version

The archetypes of harmonic syntax, and the function they play within the temporal structure of Western tonal music, are widely systematised in music theory treatises. For example, the function a chord plays within tonal structures is typically reduced to a threefold categorisation (tonic, subdominant, and dominant), whose temporal alignment carries syntactic value. In the post-tonal music of the early twentieth century, this phenomenon is far less systematised and understood, having even evolved, in recent years, towards the existence of almost as many theories and compositional systems as there are pieces or composers. In this context, the present study advances methods and tools within computational musicology that aim to uncover syntactic patterns in atonal harmonic structures. To this end, it follows recent methods by Yust (2015) on the use of phase information from the discrete Fourier transform of pitch-class sets.

The main contribution of this dissertation, extending previous research, is a three-dimensional spherical representation onto which the phase information of two discrete Fourier transform coefficients is mapped in order to trace the harmonic path of an atonal piece. The resulting spatial mappings automatically identify groups of pitch-class sets, as well as the harmonic patterns formed between them. Finally, to test this three-dimensional representation, it was used to analyse Arnold Schoenberg’s Sechs kleine Klavierstücke, Op. 19, and as a tool supporting the compositional process, suggesting standardised movements in the harmonic material presented by the composer. In summary, this study aims to: (1) arrange the harmonic sets of Schoenberg’s Op. 19 in a three-dimensional space according to their pitch classes; (2) organise these sets into distinct harmonic aggregates; (3) identify the existence of standardised movements between them; (4) compare the results of the representation with previous analyses; and (5) assess the usefulness of the system in the composer’s creative process.

The results show, among other findings, that the standardised harmonic paths within the spherical phase space tend, with each repetition, to fill the totality of pitch classes.
